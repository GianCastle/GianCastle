Chances are that at some stage of your life you've smacked yourself on the head because you didn't do things in such a way that you could get the results you expected, you've stopped to think about why things happened and how they went down... that feeling of guilt, of regret invades your head; you stopped to meditate for a moment, hours or maybe days on how things might turn out tomorrow if you do them from the countless possibilities and perspectives you imagine. Our poor brain, and yes, I say poor because we know what's going on. We enter this "blessed" state that is like ramming into a wall over and over until you dislocate your shoulder and then, after having screwed up your whole body and realizing you've achieved nothing by doing this, asking yourself why the hell were you doing it?.

While everybody overthinks things from time to time, Some People seem unable to quiet the constant barrage of thoughts. Their inner monologue includes two destructive thought patterns: ruminating and worrying.

Rumination (attention focused on the symptoms of one's own distress, and on its possible causes and consequences, as opposed to its solutions) involves rehashing the past.

> - I shouldn't have stood up and asked that nonsense. Everybody looked at me like "what's wrong with this crazy guy".
>
> - Damn... I should've stayed at my old job, I'd be happier there than here because...**any excuse would go here**
>
> - If I hadn't been so desperate maybe she would've said yes.

Worrying involves negative, often chaotic and catastrophic predictions about our uncertain future:

> - I'm going to embarrass myself tomorrow when I give that presentation. I'll turn into a wimp, my face will go <span style="color: #ff0000">#ff0000</span>, and everyone will see I'm good for nothing in the end.
>
> - I'll never get promoted. No matter what I do. That's not going to happen because my actions aren't enough because **a long list of excuses would go here**.
>
> - If this keeps up, my partner is going to find someone better than me. Nah, I'll end up more single than **write your consoling excuse**

Those who overthink don't only use words to contemplate their lives. Sometimes, they also conjure up images. They may imagine their car going off the road; we play a movie with a sad ending in our minds. Either way, that tendency of "Some People" to overthink everything keeps us from doing something productive.

Those "Some People" are us... those of us who think in excess, or Overthinkers (that's what I like to call us), are plagued by thoughts that are total delirium and we find ourselves unable to get out of that state of constant distress, we find ourselves totally blocked and locked into those causes/consequences that we believe could or could have come up as a result of what is happening or happened in our "smoked-out mind", and we lose focus on what really matters...

**Our present**

> Alright, Gian. You're telling me that we can't manage to come up with solutions to those problems that pop into our minds, that haven't even arrived or that are already gone. But then... how can we stop throwin' ourselves at that wall over and over?

<hr />

##### Instead of throwing yourself at it, climb it

<hr />

###### Notice when you're overthinking

Awareness is the first step to putting an end to overthinking. Start paying attention to the way you think. When you notice that you're replaying events in your mind over and over, or worrying about things you can't control, recognize and accept that your thoughts aren't productive. Don't wait until your shoulder hurts from having slammed into the wall a few times.

###### Challenge your thoughts (#***acceptthechallenge***)
It's super easy to get carried away by negative thoughts. Before you conclude that calling in sick to work is going to get you fired, or that you saw your girl talking to some other guy and picture yourself already wearing two 40kg horns on each side of your forehead, **recognize** that your thoughts may be exaggeratedly negative. We have to learn to recognize and replace our mind's errors before they drive us into a total frenzy.

###### Shift your focus to active problem-solving
Talking about your problems isn't helpful, but looking for solutions is. Ask yourself what steps you can take to learn from a mistake or avoid a future problem; instead of asking why something happened, ask yourself what you can do about it.

###### Change the channel
Telling yourself to stop thinking about something can backfire. The more you try to keep the thought from entering your brain, the more likely it is to keep popping up.

> Want to know one of the main reasons you overthink?
> ***It's because you have the time to do it.***

Keeping yourself busy with something is the best way to make the switch. Go to the gym, join a conversation about a completely different topic (butt in if you have to) or work on a project that distracts your mind from a flood of negative thoughts.

###### Schedule time to reflect

It isn't productive to dwell on problems for long periods of time, but a brief reflection is. For example, thinking about how you could do things differently or recognizing the potential pitfalls of a plan can help you do better in the future. Build 20 minutes of "thinking time" into your day-to-day. During this time, you let yourself worry and reflect on whatever you want. Then, when the time is up, move on to something more productive. When you notice you're overthinking outside your scheduled time, remind yourself that you'll think about it later. There's no worse time vampire than thinking. Stand firm on the idea that it will only be for that moment, no more.
