We've all heard about zeros and ones which, at the physical level, translate to one (1) for the presence of electric current and zero (0) for its absence. It's that simple… even knowing that all our processor does is simple arithmetic operations. With these two values, the complexity ends up looking alarming, and the truth is that it really is, and it's rather rare for a single man to know and understand in detail absolutely every part that makes up this complex machine. It's not so far from how the real world works, where we also have great complexity.

An example of this is a simple rubber duck I got from a coworker; if we stop to think about how much human knowledge, effort and processing chains are needed for this duck to exist. Something similar happens with computers, the difference is that the duck is something physical… we can visit the factory, learn about how plastic is obtained or how an assembly line works. In general, it's much easier to imagine what the manufacturing process of a physical product looks like in our modern world. In the case of software, however, it's quite hard to imagine, especially for someone not familiar with certain computing concepts, what Youtube, Excel or Netflix are made of or how it's possible that we play Fornite with someone on the other side of the ocean.

> The problem with software is that it's abstract, it isn't something tangible, unlike a tree.

For example, with a tree we can imagine the process that turns it into a table or a simple chair. However, from zeros and ones to what finally shows up on our screen there's a gigantic abyss, and even more so if we try to imagine the process programmers go through to create this black magic, what the secret commands are that make a screen able to show a window. As you can see, this is one of the questions that tormented me since I was a little kid. I always wanted to understand how a computer works. In particular, what caught my attention the most is how that binary code we hear so much about can turn into something visual on our screen. I tried several times to put an end to all my doubts…

The first attempt was with a Visual Basic.NET book where they explained specifically how I, just a little brat who only knew how to download games from Taringa or play the ones on Miniclip.com , could create windows, how to “program” them and bring them to life, but honestly that book was a tremendous letdown. It didn't explain what windows were, where they came from. It only told me how I could create one and customize it, how to add buttons, make those buttons perform a certain action and other kinds of “fancy stuff“ (to put it nicely), but I still had the same question…

> Where do these windows come from, who created them?

After months (maybe years, because I'm still not entirely clear on it) trying to understand many concepts from different disciplines of computing, I only managed to end up with an even
bigger mess in my head. Eventually, I discovered the famous Linux; with it I could get access to information that any old Windows couldn't give me. I even, at one point, tried to build my own Linux from scratch following a guide called Linux From Scratch and understand much better all the elements that make up an Operating System. Obviously that whole process took me a great deal of time and frustration, and while what I learned didn't directly answer all my questions, it helped me understand much better how this world works. There are many things that even today simply escape me, I don't have infinite memory, but at least I've understood where such complexity comes from and how human beings have managed to create such complex systems, and above all in so little time… The answer to everything is Layers and Layers of Abstraction.

The good thing about layers is that they exist in our tangible world and it's much easier to explain. Imagine for a moment that you're a God of architecture, someone who doesn't know what to do with their money, you have more than 25 digits in your bank account. One fine day you decide to retire, buy a piece of land and build your own house on your own. You decide to make this project a personal challenge and you complete practically the whole build; you don't deny yourself any luxury, the latest construction materials, big windows, good orientation, well insulated in terms of temperature and noise… you spend months raising walls block by block, copper pipes, electrical wiring, paint, you put in the windows and even the parking spot…

Pretty hard work, right?
It'll give you enormous satisfaction. Of course, you use a considerable amount of prefabricated elements. You even start from a fairly common house model. You complete your work with great effort and sacrifice but it was worth it… it's your own house, right? your sweat, your blind bet on your abilities for what you as a being consider your masterpiece, your final work, Your Final Boss on a game that you have been playing a long time.

You can say you've built your own home. Now imagine that one day a
caveman (we'll call him Fred Flintstone) travels from the past and lands right at your front door. He's a Homo Sapiens from the Paleolithic era. Mr. Flintstone knows nothing about our culture, he only knows the “basic technology” based on chipping little rocks and other objects he finds around him.
Mr. Flintstone (ignoring the language problem) introduces himself and asks you what these transparent rocks are, walls with regular shapes, bright colors… it doesn't resemble anything he's ever seen. You'd answer

“Well, look, this is my house. This is where I live, I built it myself. Ain't it pretty?”

Then the caveman, who has skipped a few centuries of human evolution, tries to imagine how you could have built such a masterpiece, but it simply escapes him. He can't conceive how you managed to raise such a perfect wall and color it such a uniform white. Something similar happens to us when we try to imagine how out of all those zeros and ones that simply represent presence or absence of current can come the article you're reading right now.

At this point you, aiming to calm Mr. Flintstone down, try to explain to him that you didn't really start from nothing. While it's true that you built the house yourself, you didn't manufacture the building blocks it's made of, such as windows, walls, doors and floors, or even the copper pipes or the wiring needed for electricity to reach your house… you simply acquired these materials and used them with your knowledge. Even using traditional concepts to build the house, Fred Flintstone, very puzzled, answers you…

“Then you haven't built the house. You've simply assembled preexisting blocks, bugah bugah”

You can't say you really built it. You actually can, though. At your level of
abstraction you're the one who built the house. What the caveman sees is that you contributed along with hundreds of other people to making the cement, extracting the sand needed to melt into glass, making the concrete blocks and extracting the copper from the mines; refining it so it can become wires, extracting the oil that once refined becomes the plastic that will coat the wires, even the design of the windows and frames or doors. If we get
picky, there are thousands of people who have contributed to making everything your home is made of, and really, if you think about it, you've done very little to build your home, a pretty small part, let me tell you… and yet you still think the house was made by you. From your point of view, there are many things you take for granted that you won't be able to make on your own, or the effort isn't worth it since there are products and services that provide them for you. You take for granted that in building your house we'll use building blocks such as cinder blocks. Cinder blocks are an abstract concept, for you they're a finished product that comes from another level of manufacturing; you don't know or care where they come from, how they're made or how much time or work goes into making them… you only know how much they cost and how you have to use them. This, my little friend, is a layer of abstraction.

For you the cinder block is a concept that comes to you as a given. You don't need to know all the details. In your work, the cinder block is a basic building block, and in the task of building the house there are many others like it…

In computing and in other fields this is called a layer or level of abstraction. You live happily in your world with your concepts and your basic building blocks, you build houses, while in the layer below we have civil engineers (or whoever's in charge of making a cinder block) who have no idea how a house is built but know a few things about cinder blocks, and in the layer beneath that, well, we'd have experts in extracting materials from mines so that the cinder block manufacturer can create his product, who doesn't necessarily know or care either how architects design their
products or how you build yours.

Each one lives in their own separate level, or rather, each one has abstracted themselves away from how the other levels work. Don't get me wrong, you can understand how all the levels work, but you'll certainly be better at your job if you devote yourself to thoroughly mastering your field and your level of abstraction.

The same thing happens with computers, although here there's a concept that's pretty hard to grasp…

Within digital technology, in the old days we were tied to hardware, a series of components that were specifically designed to achieve a specific result. If we wanted other results, well, we had to physically modify these circuits to make the result change. Thanks to the work of mathematicians and physicists like Ada Lovelace, John Von Neumann and Alan Turing, as well as many others, the idea was born of a single machine, the ultimate machine capable of performing calculations based on the presence or absence of
current. This is where the digital world and computing as we know it today were born.

We stop working with specific components at the hardware level, we take it for granted, and (perhaps) one of the first layers of
abstraction in computing is born, we forget about the physical components and start working with memory and calculations. The value our numbers have in these machines moves away from what the machine itself looks like. In other words, the ultimate machine capable of solving any problem…a universal machine. Really, that's not the goal of this very dense and rough article, the goal is to understand how a computer works from the lowest level of our layers, simply to understand that each level of evolution gives us a new layer of abstraction that solves many problems we'll no longer have to deal with, and we'll be able to focus on evolving while taking many things for granted.

Alright, so we have a machine that can do arithmetic operations, a next level would be to automate operations, to have the machine already know how to carry them out so that we'd just tell it [perform this sequence of actions] and it would be able to do them without any assistance. In that case, all we'd need are some input values and it would give us back output ones; something as simple as addition, then we'd move on to multiplication… If our machine can only add, knowing that a multiplication is repeating the addition of the same number a certain number of times, we could create a procedure that, knowing which numbers are involved in the multiplication, simply carries out this procedure to give us the result of the operation without us having to instruct the additions one after another.

The machine of zeros and ones; presence of current, absence of current… it receives all its instructions this way, which sounds very complicated and counterproductive, of course. But what if we define a system of letters and numbers based on codes of zeros and ones, something like Morse? Using these codes we'd create a simple language. The machine obviously doesn't understand this text, but we'd create procedures in charge of translating this text into the zeros and ones needed to perform the addition, and so, little by little, layer after layer, we arrive at modern computers. What we've just seen would be the birth of programming languages. If we move to something a bit more modern, a Graphics Driver programmer doesn't know how colors are rendered on your screen, he just sends a table of values with the color each pixel has. The screen is the one that would then take care of displaying them, since it knows how it has to talk to each physical component for the action to be carried out; in turn, the programmer who created the text console doesn't know how that color table is created, but knows that to show text he has to pass a series of specific data; in the same way, the programmer of text-based programs doesn't know how text is rendered on the screen… he only knows what he has to do to show it. Each one at the lower level has made sure the upper level has it easy, and has taken care of leaving the dirty work done. Basically, the PC or mobile device you're using right now is a black box…

It's full of black boxes, one next to the other, each level unaware of how the level above or below works internally. Right now we're talking about the lowest levels of our great mountain of layers, but if we go all the way to the top we could find, for example, a Web Programmer who just uses the browser, the text editor and a few other technologies like networks, protocols like HTTP, FTP, SFTP to create his web page and doesn't need to know
where they come from or how it works. The same thing happened with that Visual Basic .NET book, in this book we already take for granted that someone has taken care of creating the system that, using a great
number of layers underneath, is able to paint the windows on the screen, and the Visual Basic .NET programmer will only take care of configuring these windows and making them interact.

In fact, the programming language Visual Basic uses, before running, is translated into another much more complex language, which in turn is translated into another even more complex language that ends up as simple arithmetic operations on memory; In our CPU it's translated into millions of operations, and as a programmer I don't care how that happens, that's not my job. In the same way that the baker doesn't go out to the field to harvest the wheat to make the bread, that's how the world of computing and software works, and really any other extremely complex system of humanity.

First, that for a technological advance to happen, each great
contributor takes the legacy of whoever came before and improves it, and second, that every programmer, electronic or industrial engineer uses the tools and basic building blocks provided by the layer below, which is why you can say nobody knows it all. Only a small
part, and that's what lets them focus on what's really
important and do the best possible job in their area. That said, we can identify with that caveman who marvels at a work of great complexity that he isn't able to
understand. Above all, what he doesn't understand is how one man was able to do all of that; to him this man is a powerful wizard who manipulates reality, while in reality what we have is a great web of layers where thousands of people, techniques and knowledge
interact to create a masterpiece of modern engineering, just like your home or your computer.

> Now the problem looks much less overwhelming to Mr. Flintstone. The truth is, now the programmer is no longer a wizard but one more layer of our society.
